Pomona homeowners facing fire damage need a realistic sense of pricing and timeline before repairs begin.

After a fire, the two questions every Pomona homeowner asks first are how much this will cost and how long it will take to get their home back. Those answers depend on a wide range of factors, from the size of the fire to how much smoke traveled through the ductwork, but there are patterns that hold true across most homes in the area.

Pomona's housing stock includes single-story ranch homes from the 1950s and 60s, along with newer two-story builds near the eastern edge of the city. Older homes often take longer to assess because of plaster walls, older electrical systems, and materials that behave differently than modern drywall and synthetic insulation.

This guide breaks down typical cost ranges by damage severity, explains what drives those numbers up or down, and lays out a realistic week-by-week timeline for a mid-size fire damage restoration project. Pomona residents dealing with an active claim can use this as a benchmark when reviewing contractor bids and talking with their insurance adjuster.

Cost Factors That Drive the Final Number

The total cost of fire damage restoration in Pomona depends heavily on how far the fire spread before it was contained. A contained kitchen fire that stayed within one room costs far less than a fire that reached the attic or spread between floors, since attic fires often mean roof and structural framing repairs on top of interior work.

Smoke travel matters almost as much as flame damage. Even a small fire can push soot through an entire HVAC system, meaning every room in the house needs cleaning and the ductwork needs professional treatment. This single factor surprises many Pomona homeowners who expect costs limited to the room where the fire started.

Water damage from firefighting efforts adds another layer. Fire departments use significant water volume to extinguish a blaze, and that water soaks into subfloors, drywall, and insulation throughout the affected area. Drying this out properly, using industrial dehumidifiers and air movers, adds both time and expense.

Age and construction type of the Pomona property also factor in. Homes with original 1950s wiring or plumbing sometimes require code-mandated upgrades during reconstruction, since permits require bringing affected systems up to current standards rather than simply replacing what was there before.

Typical Price Ranges by Damage Severity

Minor fire damage, limited to one room with light smoke staining and no structural harm, generally runs a modest range in Pomona once cleaning, deodorizing, and minor drywall repair are included. This category often covers small kitchen or appliance fires caught quickly.

Moderate damage, where fire spread beyond the room of origin or smoke reached adjacent living spaces, pushes costs meaningfully higher. This tier usually includes full duct cleaning, replacement of damaged flooring, drywall removal and repair in multiple rooms, and repainting throughout affected areas.

Severe damage, involving structural framing, roof penetration, or a fire that affected most of the home, represents the highest cost tier. These projects often blur the line between restoration and full reconstruction, sometimes requiring the homeowner to relocate for months while the Pomona property is rebuilt from the studs out in the affected sections.

Content restoration, covering cleaning of furniture, clothing, and personal belongings, is typically a separate line item from structural repair. Some items can be cleaned and salvaged offsite, while heavily charred or water-logged items usually get replaced through the insurance claim rather than restored.

Week One: Emergency Mitigation and Assessment

The first days after a fire in Pomona focus on stopping further damage rather than starting repairs. A restoration crew typically arrives within hours to board up windows, tarp damaged roof sections, and secure the property against weather and unauthorized entry.

Water extraction begins immediately if firefighting efforts left standing water or soaked materials. Industrial extraction equipment removes bulk water within the first day, followed by placement of dehumidifiers and air movers to begin the drying process across affected rooms.

A detailed damage assessment happens during this window as well, with the restoration company documenting every affected area through photos and moisture readings. This documentation becomes the foundation for both the insurance claim and the eventual repair scope of work.

By the end of the first week, most Pomona properties have moved from emergency stabilization into the planning phase, with an initial estimate ready for the insurance adjuster's review and a rough timeline established for the homeowner.

Weeks Two Through Four: Demolition and Drying

Once the structure is stabilized, the demolition phase begins. This means removing damaged drywall, insulation, flooring, and any charred framing that cannot be salvaged. In Pomona homes with plaster construction, this phase sometimes takes longer since plaster removal is more labor intensive than standard drywall.

Drying continues throughout this period, with daily moisture readings tracking progress in wall cavities and subfloors. A structure typically needs to reach a specific moisture threshold before rebuilding can start, since trapping moisture behind new drywall risks mold growth down the line.

Odor treatment usually happens toward the end of this phase, once porous materials that trapped smoke smell have been removed. Sealing agents and thermal fogging treat the remaining structure to neutralize odor before new materials go in.

This period is often the least visible to homeowners, since much of the work involves waiting for drying equipment to do its job rather than active construction. Patience here pays off, since rushing past proper drying causes problems that resurface months later in the finished Pomona home.

Weeks Four Through Eight: Reconstruction Begins

With the structure dry and demolition complete, reconstruction starts. This phase includes framing repair, new insulation, drywall installation, and any electrical or plumbing work needed to meet current code. Pomona's permitting process through the city building department applies here, and permit timing can add days to the schedule depending on inspector availability.

Flooring, cabinetry, and trim work typically follow drywall completion. For moderate damage projects, this phase often represents the bulk of the visible progress homeowners see, as rooms start looking like livable spaces again rather than construction sites.

Painting and finishing touches close out this phase for most rooms. Contractors typically prime with an odor-blocking sealer before final paint coats, adding an extra layer of protection against any residual smoke smell that escaped earlier treatment.

For a moderate damage project in Pomona, this reconstruction phase often runs four to six weeks, though severe structural damage can extend this considerably longer depending on the scope of framing and roofing work required.

Content Restoration Running in Parallel

While structural work proceeds, content restoration typically happens on a separate track. Salvageable furniture, clothing, and personal items get packed out early in the process and transported to a cleaning facility rather than staying onsite through demolition and construction dust.

Textiles like clothing and linens go through specialized cleaning processes designed to remove smoke odor at a molecular level rather than just masking it. Electronics require careful evaluation, since soot residue is corrosive and can cause delayed failures even in items that appear to power on normally after a fire.

Photographs, documents, and sentimental items often need the most delicate handling, sometimes requiring freeze-drying techniques if they were also water damaged. Pomona families should ask their restoration company early about which items qualify for salvage attempts versus which are better handled through insurance replacement.

This parallel timeline means content often returns to the home around the same time structural work finishes, allowing for a more coordinated move back rather than piecemeal delivery over many weeks.

Final Walkthrough and Move-Back Timeline

The final phase involves a detailed walkthrough with the homeowner, contractor, and sometimes the insurance adjuster together. This is the time to flag any concerns before signing off on completed work, since addressing issues after final payment becomes more difficult.

Deep cleaning of the entire property happens just before move-back, removing construction dust and giving every surface a final pass. HVAC systems get a final filter change and system check to confirm no lingering odor remains in the air circulation.

For a moderate damage Pomona home, total time from fire to move-back typically runs eight to twelve weeks. Minor damage projects can finish in three to four weeks, while severe structural damage projects sometimes extend past six months when major rebuilding is involved.

Homeowners should build in a buffer beyond the contractor's initial estimate, since permit delays, material backorders, or discovered hidden damage commonly push timelines by a week or two even on well-run projects.

Getting the Most Accurate Estimate for Your Home

The best way to get an accurate cost and timeline estimate is to have at least two contractors walk the property in person rather than quoting from photos alone. Hidden damage behind walls or in the attic often only becomes clear during a hands-on inspection.

Ask each contractor to break down their estimate by phase, mitigation, demolition, reconstruction, and content restoration, rather than providing one lump sum. This makes it far easier to compare bids and understand where costs are concentrated.

Confirm whether the estimate includes permit fees, since these vary by project scope and are sometimes overlooked in initial numbers. Permit costs through Pomona's building department can add a noticeable amount depending on the extent of electrical, plumbing, and structural work involved.

Finally, ask how the contractor handles supplemental costs if additional damage is discovered mid-project. A clear process for documenting and approving these changes keeps the budget from spiraling unexpectedly as work progresses on the Pomona property.
