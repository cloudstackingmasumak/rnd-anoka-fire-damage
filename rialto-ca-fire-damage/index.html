Timeline, Secondary Drying, and Moisture Barriers

The drying timeline affects your ability to occupy the home. If structural drying is ongoing, the home might not be habitable due to equipment noise, disruption, and moisture. Many families stay elsewhere during this phase. Your insurance usually covers temporary housing costs during this time, so discuss this with your adjuster. Some materials dry faster than others. Hardwood floors might dry in two weeks, while carpet padding takes longer. Insulation and framing take the longest because water is trapped deep within. Professionals manage the drying process to address different materials appropriately.

In Rialto, CA, we also consider that masonry may be exposed to salt air and coastal moisture, which complicates drying. Salt deposits in porous masonry can draw moisture and prevent complete drying. Complete structural drying typically takes one to four weeks depending on the amount of water, the materials affected, and the climate. Drying is slower in humid conditions or with high water volumes. Professionals provide estimates based on their assessment of water damage severity. After primary drying is complete, secondary drying addresses moisture that remains in cavities, behind walls, and in other spaces not directly accessible. This phase might involve drilling access holes, using moisture barriers, or applying treatments that prevent residual moisture from causing problems.

Mold growth causes structural damage that's difficult and expensive to repair. Mold digests wood, breaking down the cellulose that gives wood its strength. Over months and years, structural members become weakened and eventually fail. Mold also produces mycotoxins that trigger respiratory problems, allergies, and other health issues. People in Rialto homes with hidden mold sometimes report persistent respiratory symptoms that don't improve until mold is addressed. By the time mold damage is visible or growth is detectable, significant structural compromise has usually already occurred.

How Much Water Firefighting Actually Uses

Modern fire engines carry thousands of gallons of water. A typical residential fire might require ten thousand to thirty thousand gallons of water to extinguish, depending on the size of the fire and how long it burns. That is not a small amount. That is the equivalent of filling several swimming pools' worth of water inside and around your Rialto home.

Where does all that water go? Most of it flows out as the fire is extinguished, but a substantial portion absorbs into walls, flooring, and materials. Some collects in basements and low areas. Some soaks into insulation, drywall, and wood framing. This absorbed water is the problem you have to solve.

Industrial dehumidifiers are the core of structural drying. These machines are not household dehumidifiers. They remove 100 to 300 pints of water per day, running continuously for weeks. Multiple units are placed throughout the home to dry different areas simultaneously.

Air movers (large fans) circulate air around and through damp materials. Moving air accelerates evaporation. These fans operate 24/7 and are loud, which is why families often leave the home during drying.

Heat sometimes accelerates drying, but it's used carefully. Raising temperature too much can cause some materials to warp or crack. Professionals balance temperature and humidity to optimize evaporation without damaging materials.

Drying Equipment and Techniques

Moisture extraction in sensitive areas like attics and crawlspaces requires careful equipment placement. If a dehumidifier is not removing enough moisture, we add additional units or adjust placement. If air movers are creating pressure imbalances, we reposition them. Water from firefighting does not just wet surfaces. It penetrates deep into materials. Drywall absorbs water like a sponge. The water travels through the drywall and into the insulation behind it. It soaks into wood studs and framing. It reaches into cavities and voids you cannot see.
